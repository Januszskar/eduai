Six Core Principles for AI Use

These five principles (plus pedagogical soundness) underpin every AI-related decision in the Faculty. Work through each tab.

Five principles, explored

AI use must support genuine learning without facilitating plagiarism, cheating or misrepresentation. This means being transparent about AI use, properly citing AI-generated assistance, verifying accuracy, and maintaining fairness. Transparency and traceability are supported through plagiarism/similarity declarations and disclosure of AI platforms used.

AI integration must not disadvantage any group. Digital access and AI literacy vary widely in a resource-diverse context like South Africa — instructors must provide orientation and ongoing support. Stay vigilant about bias: users should critically evaluate outputs for gender, cultural or racial bias, and the Faculty favours tools assessed for fairness in a Global South context.

Every user must keep an auditable record of AI-assisted tasks — time-stamped prompt histories producible on request. Failure to supply logs when required may constitute a breach of academic integrity. Humans, not algorithms, are ultimately accountable: every AI-assisted action needs an identifiable, responsible human party.

Never enter personal, sensitive or confidential information into public AI services. Comply with POPIA, UWC's Data Protection Policy and HSSREC ethical protocols; use only approved, secure platforms for institutional data. Tools like Napkin AI and NotebookLM are noted as ones that let IP remain with the producer.

AI should support — not substitute — core learning outcomes. It may help brainstorm, refine drafts, or analyse data, but must not replace critical thinking, creativity or subject engagement. Always cross-verify AI-provided facts, data and references against authoritative sources, and confirm every citation actually exists.

Three ways this plays out in practice

  • Freeing time for engagement: automate routine grading to create space for mentoring, guided lab work and project consultations.
  • Supporting deeper inquiry: students use AI as a tutor or study aid — not as the author of their work.
  • Maintaining oversight & quality: any AI-generated content used in teaching or assessment must be reviewed by a human for accuracy and relevance.
